Wave Grid 3×3: design brief

University of Scranton Amateur Radio Club (W3USR) · Project lead: Nathaniel A. Frissell, W2NAF · 21 November 2026

Goal

This brief describes the Wave Grid 3×3, a 3D-printed board that lets a blind student feel how amplitude and frequency change the shape of a signal. The students are blind or visually impaired adults preparing for the US Technician Class amateur radio license through the Courage Kenny Handiham Program.

We would like the builder's help making it a finished, tested aid: review the design, print it, and refine it after the first touch tests. The board covers two of the four Week 2 concepts, amplitude and frequency. Wavelength and phase come later.

The Wave Grid 3×3

A 3×3 grid of 2-inch sine-wave tiles teaches amplitude and wave shape by touch. Columns step up in frequency and rows step down in amplitude. The x axis is time, as on an oscilloscope trace, and the wave is extruded 2 inches so the fingers have room to feel it.

Every tile starts at the same phase, so the tiles differ only in frequency and amplitude. Nothing has been printed yet. The first print should check whether 0.2 mm layer steps are felt on the 1.5 mm tiles.

How a student uses it

The board sits flat on the table with the orientation dot at the student's front left. An instructor guides the first pass; after that, the student can explore alone.

  1. Orient. Find the raised dot at the front-left corner and square up the board.
  2. Read the labels. The back strip names the frequency axis and each column. The left strip names the amplitude axis and each row. The front strip says “time,” with an arrow pointing the way time runs.
  3. Find the reference. Trace the rim. It sits at the zero level, so crests rise above it and troughs dip below it.
  4. Compare frequencies. Trace the back row from left to right. The crests stay equally tall, and each tile packs in more of them: 1, then 2, then 4 cycles.
  5. Compare amplitudes. Trace the left column from back to front. Each tile has one cycle, and the crests shrink: 6, then 3, then 1.5 mm.
  6. Separate the two ideas. Compare the back-left tile (one tall cycle) with the front-right tile (four small cycles). Height is amplitude; crowding is frequency.

Limits of the analogy

The board shows frequency as spacing along a time axis, and the student's own hand supplies the time. That makes it an imperfect analogy in four ways:

  1. The hand sets the rate. Sweeping a tile quickly or slowly changes the rate the fingertip feels, so two tiles can feel alike.
  2. The same shape is the textbook picture of wavelength. With x read as distance, these tiles show wavelength, and the exam tests the inverse relationship between the two.
  3. The fingertip feels period. Crest spacing is time per cycle; frequency is its inverse, one step removed.
  4. Steepness mixes the variables. Slope grows with frequency × amplitude, so a student may read sharpness as either one.

The board remains a good tool for amplitude and wave shape. Relabeling the axis as distance later lets the same tiles teach wavelength. Three inexpensive steps soften the frequency limits:

  • A Braille time scale. “1 second per tile” makes the columns read as 1, 2, and 4 Hz, a number in place of an impression.
  • Name the period. Teach crest spacing as time per cycle, so “closer bumps” means “more cycles in the same second.”
  • Pair it with sound. A tone whose pitch rises with frequency links the board to what the student already hears.

A moving demonstrator would remove these limits; it is set aside for now.

Open questions

Design and printing:

  • Amplitude steps. Are 6, 3, and 1.5 mm clearly distinct by touch? Is 1.5 mm too subtle, or 6 mm too sharp at 4 cycles?
  • Surface. Will 0.2 mm layer steps be felt on the gentle tiles? Options: 0.1 mm layers, ironing, or light sanding.
  • Grooves. Do 4 mm grooves mark tile boundaries clearly without catching a fingertip?
  • Printed Braille. Do the printed dots read cleanly by touch, and does the raised print stay legible? If the dots print poorly, Dymo Braille labels on the same strips are the fallback.
  • Printer. The labeled board is 230 × 226 mm. If the available printer bed is smaller, the label strips can print as separate pieces that pin onto the base.
  • Material. PLA or PETG, and which finish feels best?
  • One piece or tiles. Print the board whole, or as separate tiles that drop into a frame so they can be swapped and rearranged?
