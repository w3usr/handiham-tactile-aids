Feeling a Wave: Activity Guide

Wave Grid 3×3, for a teacher working with a student who is blind or has low vision

About this activity

The student explores the Wave Grid, a 3D-printed board with nine wave tiles, and learns two properties of a wave by touch. By the end, the student can:

  1. Describe amplitude as how high a wave’s crests rise above its middle line.
  2. Describe frequency as how many cycles happen in a fixed time, so that more cycles in the same space means a higher frequency.
  3. Tell amplitude and frequency apart on any tile.

Plan on about 30 to 45 minutes, one-on-one. The questions in Part 5 can also serve as a short assessment at the end, or at the start of a later session.

What you need

  • The Wave Grid board, flat on a table, with the edge that says “time” toward the student.
  • Optional: a way to play a steady tone at different pitches and loudness levels, for the sound connection in Part 4.

Know the board before you start

The board has nine tiles in three rows and three columns, surrounded by a flat rim.

  • Rows set the amplitude. The back row, farthest from the student, has the tallest waves (6 mm crests). The middle row has 3 mm crests, and the front row 1.5 mm.
  • Columns set the frequency. Every tile in the left column holds 1 cycle. The middle column holds 2 cycles, and the right column 4.
  • The rim is the zero level. Crests rise above it and troughs dip below it, so it is the reference for feeling amplitude.
  • Labels are raised print with Braille underneath. The back strip says “frequency” and gives each column’s cycles. The left strip says “amplitude” in its back corner and gives each row’s crest height beside the row. The front strip says “time,” with a raised arrow pointing to the right.
  • An orientation dot, larger than a Braille dot, marks the front-left corner.

Every tile starts at its middle line and rises first, so tiles differ only in amplitude and frequency.

Name tiles by position and use the same words every time: “back left,” “middle center,” “front right,” and so on. Let the student’s hands lead. Ask before guiding the student’s hands, and describe what is coming before the student touches it.

Part 1: Get oriented

  1. Say: “This board has nine small tiles, each with a wave you can feel. First, let’s find the edges. Can you find a raised dot near one corner?”
  2. When the student finds the orientation dot, say: “That dot marks the front-left corner. Keep it at your left hand, nearest you.”
  3. Have the student trace the rim all the way around, then find the three strips with labels: back, left, and front. If the student reads Braille, let them read each label. If not, read the labels aloud as the student touches them.

Listen for: the student can return to the dot and name the four edges without help.

Part 2: Amplitude

  1. Guide the student to the back-left tile. Say: “Run one finger slowly from left to right. You will feel the wave go up, come back down, go below the rim, and come back up.”
  2. Say: “The highest point is called a crest. The lowest point is called a trough. Find the crest. How far above the rim does it rise?”
  3. Move down the left column, one tile at a time, repeating the trace. Ask: “What changed? What stayed the same?”
  4. Introduce the word: “How high the crest rises above the middle line is called the amplitude. The back tile has the largest amplitude.” Have the student read the row labels: 6 mm, 3 mm, 1.5 mm.

Listen for: “the bumps got lower,” “flatter,” or “smaller,” and “it’s still one bump.” Both halves matter: the amplitude changed and the number of cycles did not.

Part 3: Frequency

  1. Return to the back-left tile. Say: “Count the crests from left to right.” (One.)
  2. Move right along the back row, counting the crests on each tile. (Two, then four.)
  3. Ask: “What changed? What stayed the same?”
  4. Say: “One full up-and-down is called a cycle. Pretend each tile shows exactly one second of a signal. Then this tile has 1 cycle each second, this one 2, and this one 4. The number of cycles each second is the frequency, measured in hertz. The right-hand tile has the highest frequency, 4 hertz.” Have the student read the column labels.

Listen for: “more bumps,” “closer together,” and “same height.” If the student says the right-hand tile is “faster,” ask what would make it faster, and steer toward “more cycles in the same second.”

Part 4: Put it together

  1. Ask the student to find tiles from a description:
    • “Find the tile with tall waves and only one cycle.” (Back left.)
    • “Find the tile with the smallest waves and the most cycles.” (Front right.)
    • “Find a tile with medium waves and two cycles.” (Middle center.)
  2. Have the student compare the back-left and front-right tiles with one hand on each. Say: “Height is amplitude. How crowded the crests are is frequency. Which is which here?”
  3. Optional sound connection. Play a quiet tone and then a loud tone at the same pitch: “Same frequency, bigger amplitude.” Then play a low and a high pitch at the same loudness: “Same amplitude, higher frequency.” Ask the student which row and which column each sound belongs to.

Listen for: the student finds each tile without trial and error, and explains the difference in their own words.

Part 5: Questions to think about

Ask these after the student has explored. Notes after each question say what a strong answer includes. Let the student use the board while answering.

Check understanding

  1. On this board, what is amplitude? A strong answer: how high the crests rise above the rim, or the middle line.
  2. What is frequency? A strong answer: how many cycles happen each second; on the board, how many crests fit in one tile.
  3. Which tile has both the largest amplitude and the highest frequency? Answer: back right.

Reason it out

  1. Suppose a new tile had 8 cycles. How would it feel compared with the back-right tile? A strong answer: twice as many crests, packed twice as close together.
  2. Can a wave have a large amplitude and a low frequency? Show me one. Answer: yes, the back-left tile.
  3. Run your finger slowly across the back-right tile, then quickly across the back-left tile. The bumps can come just as fast. Does that make their frequencies the same? A strong answer: no. Each tile stands for the same one second, and the back-right tile fits four cycles into it. Moving a finger faster changes how fast you read the tile, not the wave.

Connect it

  1. If each tile were a sound, which would be loudest? Which would have the highest pitch? A strong answer: the back row is loudest, because loudness goes with amplitude. The right column has the highest pitch, because pitch goes with frequency.
  2. Radio signals are waves too. A signal at 146 megahertz goes through 146 million cycles every second. Why might a radio need to tell frequencies apart? An open question: many stations share the air at once, and each uses its own frequency, so a radio picks one by its frequency.

Notes for the teacher

  • What the board cannot show. A tile is a frozen picture. The student’s finger supplies the motion, so how fast the crests arrive depends on how fast the student moves. Question 6 brings this out on purpose. Treat it as a chance to talk about models: the tile stands for one second of a signal, whatever speed the finger travels.
  • Braille and print. The labels are Unified English Braille, Grade 1 (uncontracted), with raised print above for students with low vision. For a student who does not read Braille, read the labels aloud.
  • This guide comes as a web page, a printable PDF, and a Braille-ready file (BRF, contracted Grade 2), so a teacher or student who reads Braille can use it directly.
